Notes: homa-lab tests the Linux kernel module that implements Homa, a datacenter transport protocol from Stanford designed as an alternative to TCP for remote procedure calls. The lab runs the module on four hosts joined by a switch with real priority queues, and measures what Homa does under load, under incast and under loss.
What the lab does
Behaviour and correctness, not datacenter benchmarks
Pinned build
Mainline kernel 6.17.8 and a pinned HomaModule commit. One command builds the image and runs the module's 840 unit tests.
A real switch
sw1 shapes each port to 1 Gbit/s with 8 strict priority queues chosen by the DSCP bits Homa sets, or one FIFO.
Homa's own tools
homa_test, the echo server and cp_node from the module's util/, driven over SSH; a Homa dissector for tshark.
30 tests
Robot suites: platform, function v4/v6, the wire, Homa vs TCP, incast, loss recovery, timers, offload.

Notes: The four things the lab is built around. It tests behaviour and correctness, not raw performance: these are VMs on one 12-core host, so absolute numbers are not datacenter numbers, but comparisons on the same machines are fair.
PART 1
Homa for dummies
What problem Homa solves, how it works, and what it costs — no networking background needed.

Notes: This part is for people who don't live in transport protocols. If the audience knows TCP well, it can be skipped to part 2.
The problem is the slowest message
One click fans out to hundreds of small requests; the page waits for the last answer

Datacenter traffic is mostly tiny messages — a few hundred bytes — mixed with occasional big transfers. With TCP the tiny ones get stuck in queues behind the big ones.
What has to improve is the tail (the 99th percentile), not the average.
4
Notes: The key idea: a request to a datacenter service fans out to many servers and finishes only when the slowest answer arrives. If 1 message in 100 is slow and a page needs 100 of them, 1 - 0.99^100 = 63% of pages are slow. So what matters is the tail, the 99th percentile, not the average.
A road versus a loading dock
Who decides when data moves, and whether small things can overtake
TCP: a single-lane road
Each sender opens a connection and streams bytes. Drivers accelerate until there is a crash (a lost packet), then brake. Queues fill up before anyone slows down. A pizza scooter behind a convoy of trucks waits behind all of them.
Homa: a dock with a dispatcher
Small parcels drive straight in on an express lane. Anyone with a truckload phones ahead; the dispatcher, who sees everything coming, tells each truck when to come — the one with the least left to unload goes first. Nobody crashes into a full queue, and the scooter never waits behind a truck.
5
Notes: The analogy. TCP: every sender accelerates until something is lost, then brakes; small messages queue behind big ones. Homa: the receiver acts as a dispatcher that sees everything heading its way, and the network has express lanes for small messages.
How Homa works, in five ideas
1
Messages, not streams
Whole messages in, whole messages out: request + response = an RPC.
2
No connections
One socket talks to thousands of peers with no setup or teardown.
3
Small messages just go
Up to ~40 KB is sent at once; a longer message is announced and waits.
4
The receiver dispatches
It grants permission to send, closest-to-finishing first.
5
Express lanes in the switch
Each packet carries a priority 0–7; switches serve 7 first.
6
Notes: The five ideas, in order. Note idea 3 in this module version: messages up to about 40 KB go immediately; since September 2026 a longer message is announced with START_MSG and every byte waits for a grant.
What Homa gives you
Low tail latency
Papers report 10–100× lower p99 for short messages under load than TCP.
No head-of-line blocking
Messages are independent; one slow message doesn't hold up the rest.
Incast by design
When 100 servers answer at once, the receiver lets them in a few at a time.
Scales to many peers
No connection per pair: no handshakes, no connection tables.
Fits RPC software
Gives frameworks messages directly instead of a byte stream.
Uses existing hardware
Switch priority queues already exist; they just need configuring.
7
Notes: Benefits. The 10-100x figure is the Homa authors' measurement on real datacenter hardware; the lab reproduces the direction of the effect at small scale: under incast, Homa's short messages stayed under 2 ms at p99 while TCP's took 20-40 ms.
What it costs
Not in Linux yet
Out-of-tree module tied to kernel versions; upstreaming in stages since 2024.
Apps must change
Message API, mapped receive buffers, 1 MB limit. Not a drop-in for TCP.
Datacenter only
No encryption; NAT, firewalls and load balancers don't know protocol 146.
Needs switch config
Without priority queues much of the benefit is gone.
Offload is fragile
Hardware TSO is TCP-only. Homa's GSO hit a host limit here, its TCP disguise a module bug.
Big messages can starve
Shortest-first favours small ones; the FIFO-share knob is off by default.
Tuned for 25–100 Gbit/s
Default timers misfired on this lab's 1 Gbit/s links.
A young ecosystem
Few tools and few deployments; even its dissector was stale.
8
Notes: Drawbacks, several measured in this lab: without priority queues the short-message advantage shrinks about 10x at the median; TCP wins big transfers here (not because of offload: the gap stayed with offload working for Homa); the default timers misfire at 1 Gbit/s. grant_fifo_fraction reserves bandwidth for the oldest message and is 0 (off) in this build.

Notes: When to use which. Homa makes sense for RPC-heavy services inside one datacenter whose owner controls hosts and switches, where tail latency is the problem. Across the internet, for bulk transfer, or with apps that can't change, TCP or QUIC remains the answer.

Notes: The lab's own finding. Shortest-first grants park all but the leading message at low priorities; strict priority starves them for tens of milliseconds at 1 Gbit/s. After 5 ms of silence (resend_ticks) the receiver asks for data that is only queued; the copies overflow the same queue and RPCs time out. Homa's defaults assume 25-100 Gbit/s. Scaling resend_ticks, resend_interval and timeout_ticks by 10 removes the effect.

What we learned
Priority queues work
Under incast Homa's short messages stayed at 0.31 ms median; one FIFO: 2.99 ms; TCP: 13+ ms.
Scale the timers
At 1 Gbit/s the default resend timer misfires; ×10 removes drops and timeouts.
Offload, two causes
GSO frames die on the lab host (no Homa there); TCP hijacking hit a module bug, patched.
Lab plumbing matters
UDP tunnels lost Homa's bursts silently; captures must be taken on the switch.
21
Notes: Summary of the findings. The priority-queue result reproduces Homa's central claim at small scale. The timer finding matters for anyone running Homa on slower links or emulating it. The offload and ecosystem points are the practical costs of adopting it today.
